## TL;DR

1. **AI is moving faster than most institutions are planning for.** The UK's own AI Security Institute (AISI) reports frontier models "far surpassed PhD-level expertise in biology". It also says cyber task success rose from 9% to 50% in about two years. Forecaster aggregates now put "AGI" around the early 2030s, though the range is wide. The human's view, that AI and robots could be doing nearly everything within 10–15 years (2036–2041), sits inside the range of serious forecasts. It is earlier than the average academic survey.
2. **The jobs impact has started, mainly through *not hiring* rather than mass firing.** UK vacancies "nearly halved since their 2022 peak". Government analysis finds AI-exposed occupations saw steeper drops in job postings. Morgan Stanley found UK firms using AI recorded "an 8% net job loss", roughly double the international average. Mid-career workers (2–10 years' experience) are hit as well as entry-level ones. Official bodies stress that AI is not yet proven to be the *cause* of the wider slowdown.
3. **The government has moved from "no job losses overall" to contingency planning.** The new AI minister says Britain is preparing for possible "unprecedented" job losses. But the main concrete worker measure in Budget 2026 is **£10m a year to study** the impact. That is not a support plan.
4. **Value is leaking abroad.** Less than £1 in every £6 of public tech spending with big suppliers goes to British firms. Stargate UK (£31bn) was paused over energy costs. The Sovereign AI Unit has up to £500m, which is small next to private US budgets.
5. **The public is worried, distrusts both AI firms and government, and doesn't believe gains will be shared.** Seven in 10 are worried about AI's economic impacts. Only 7% think the benefits will be shared fairly. 79% have little or no confidence in AI companies. Only 9% trust politicians to tell the truth.
6. **There is a clear political gap.** No UK party makes "managing the AI transition fairly" its central purpose:
   - Labour (now under PM Andy Burnham) is studying and planning.
   - The Conservatives and Reform focus on deregulation.
   - The Lib Dems focus on digital sovereignty, and the Greens on regulation and public ownership.
   - Safety campaigners (ControlAI) have 125+ parliamentarians but focus on superintelligence, not jobs.
   - Economic think tanks (IPPR, TUC, Windfall Trust) have the ideas but no electoral vehicle.
7. **Similar parties exist abroad, not in the UK.** Japan's Team Mirai, an AI engineer's party founded in 2025, won 11 lower-house seats in February 2026 under proportional representation. Denmark's Synthetic Party was an art project. In the UK, nothing comparable is registered.
8. **Lessons for a single-issue party:**
   - UKIP won its issue by pressuring the big parties, then died of its own success.
   - Reform survived by broadening.
   - The SDP shows how first-past-the-post punishes evenly spread support.
   - A credible AI party must be trusted on everything else and attract experienced people. It also needs a long-term reason to exist beyond any single win.
9. **Implications for the Beacons:** the evidence supports short-dated, measurable Beacons:
   - a **transition income guarantee** for displaced middle-income workers;
   - a **UK AI stake/investment** target;
   - a **public AI dividend fund**;
   - an **honest-tracking** mechanism, since trust is the scarcest resource in UK politics;
   - a long-range **Universal High Income** destination.
10. **Some measures only work internationally** (added in v1.1).
    - Acting alone on AI taxes or strict licensing risks driving investment away: Stargate UK was paused, and the US has threatened tariffs over the UK's digital services tax.
    - Coordination is possible but fragile. The OECD global minimum tax was agreed, then US multinationals were largely exempted.
    - The opening is real: the UK holds the **G20 presidency in 2027**, and PM Burnham proposes a "Manchester Compact on AI". Meanwhile 22 countries led by Finland and Norway have called for a global AI oversight body.
    - This supports a **Global Treaty Beacon**, built on the rule: go first where it's safe to act alone, and coordinate where acting alone would drive investment away.

## A. How fast AI is moving

### A1. Current capability

The UK **AI Security Institute (AISI)** published its first *Frontier AI Trends Report* in December 2025, covering two years of government testing.

> "Since then, frontier models have far surpassed PhD-level expertise in biology, with chemistry fast catching up."
> — [AISI, *5 key findings from our first Frontier AI Trends Report*, 18 Dec 2025](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/5-key-findings-from-our-first-frontier-ai-trends-report)

> "In late 2023, models could only complete apprentice-level cyber tasks 9% of the time. Today, this figure is 50%."
> — [AISI, 18 Dec 2025](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/5-key-findings-from-our-first-frontier-ai-trends-report)

**METR**, a non-profit evaluator, measures the length of tasks AI agents can complete autonomously (their "time horizon"):
- In 2025 it reported this had doubled roughly every seven months since 2019.
- Third-party analysis of METR's January 2026 update suggested an even faster pace (about 10x per year). That figure is a secondary interpretation, not METR's own.
- METR cautions that its tasks are mostly software, ML and cyber, and that other domains will differ:

> "we expect that time horizons on all economically valuable tasks will range over several orders of magnitude."
> — [METR, *Task-Completion Time Horizons of Frontier AI Models*, last updated 8 Sep 2026](https://metr.org/time-horizons/)

### A2. Forecasts and timelines

| Source | What it says | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Metaculus community (early 2026, via secondary summaries) | ~25% chance of AGI by 2029, ~50% by 2033 | [80,000 Hours review](https://80000hours.org/agi/guide/when); numbers update continuously |
| Samotsvety forecasters (Jan 2026) | 50% chance of AGI by 2041 | Via secondary summaries |
| AI Impacts survey of 1,714 AI researchers (2023) | 50% chance of "high-level machine intelligence" by 2047 | Academic researchers tend to be later than forecasters |
| Calum Chace (*The Economic Singularity*) | Economic singularity: "2040 seems a very reasonable possibility" † | [Chace via Goodreads/Forbes](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/26120547-the-economic-singularity-and-the-five-as?tab=author) |
| UK Government Office for Science, *AI Scenarios 2030* (June 2026) | Five scenarios, from "Slow Burn" to "Take-Off" | Explicitly not predictions |

The UK government's own foresight work treats rapid transformation as a live possibility:

> "They are not predictions, and the future may involve elements from all scenarios."
> "AI could cause serious, potentially even existential harms, without government intervention."
> — [GO-Science, *AI Scenarios 2030*, 15 Jun 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-scenarios-2030-helping-policymakers-plan-for-the-future-of-ai/ai-scenarios-2030-helping-policymakers-plan-for-the-future-of-ai)

**Assessment of the human's 10–15 year view.** A world where AI and robots are doing "nearly everything" by 2036–2041 is:
- **inside** the range of serious forecasts. The forecaster median for "AGI" is early 2030s, and Chace says ~2040 for the economic singularity.
- **earlier** than academic surveys (2047).
- **subject to a robotics lag.** Physical automation needs hardware, factories and deployment, which move more slowly than software. A realistic Beacon plan should assume cognitive work is disrupted first (now to ~2032) and physical work later (~2030s onward).

The **case against** fast timelines comes mainly from:
- economists who see diffusion as slow;
- the Bank of England's finding that most firms haven't yet changed headcount (see B1);
- METR's own caveats about narrow task sets.

These are reasons to build Beacons that **adapt**, not reasons to wait.

## B. Jobs and the UK workforce

### B1. What the evidence shows so far

**Hiring is falling, especially in AI-exposed occupations:**

> "UK vacancies nearly halved since their 2022 peak"
> "Occupational signals alone cannot tell us whether the weakening in hiring can be partially explained by AI, remote working, or both."
> — [Bank Underground (Bank of England staff blog), *Canaries in the column?*, 6 Aug 2026](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2026/08/06/canaries-in-the-column-ai-exposure-and-the-uks-hiring-slowdown/)

> "a one standard deviation increase in AI exposure was associated with a 3.9% reduction in posting volume"
> "These patterns are suggestive, but do not yet establish that AI is the cause of the decline"
> — [DSIT & AISI, *Assessment of AI capabilities and the impact on the UK labour market*, 28 Jan 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/assessment-of-ai-capabilities-and-the-impact-on-the-uk-labour-market/assessment-of-ai-capabilities-and-the-impact-on-the-uk-labour-market)

> "Around 70% of UK workers are in occupations containing tasks that AI could potentially perform or enhance"
> — [DSIT & AISI, 28 Jan 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/assessment-of-ai-capabilities-and-the-impact-on-the-uk-labour-market/assessment-of-ai-capabilities-and-the-impact-on-the-uk-labour-market) (citing IMF estimates)

**The UK appears to be worse hit than peers.** In January 2026, Morgan Stanley research covered firms that had used AI for at least a year in five sectors:

> "UK firms recorded an 8% net job loss linked to AI over the past year"
> "While American firms used those gains to expand hiring, British companies did not."
> — [eWeek reporting Morgan Stanley/Bloomberg, 28 Jan 2026](https://www.eweek.com/news/ai-job-losses-uk-morgan-stanley-report/)

Caveats: the study covers five AI-exposed sectors, not the whole economy, and its authors describe it as a downside scenario. Coverage notes mid-career professionals (2–10 years' experience) were among those most affected, not just entry-level workers.

**Employers are already acting:**

> "A fifth of employers (22%) have already made roles redundant or reduced hiring because of AI"
> — [King's College London Policy Institute, 19 May 2026](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/one-in-five-britons-think-ai-will-create-civil-unrest-study-finds)

**The official view is "early signs, not proof":**

> "Looking ahead, businesses expect AI to reduce employment slightly over the next few years."
> "However, there is limited evidence that AI is the main reason for this decline."
> — [Bank of England, *How will AI affect jobs and wages?*](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/how-will-ai-affect-jobs-and-wages)

**Graduate data is mixed.** Adzuna reported UK graduate listings fell 45% year on year to below 10,000 in January 2026 ([techUK](https://www.techuk.org/events/entry-level-and-graduate-jobs-in-the-era-of-ai)). By contrast, the Institute of Student Employers found graduate recruitment *rose* 4% in 2026 after an 8% fall in 2025.

### B2. Who is most exposed

IPPR's 2024 modelling remains the most-cited UK estimate:

> "11 per cent of tasks done by workers are already exposed to in the first wave"
> "Central scenario: 4.4 million jobs disappear, but with economic gains of 6.3 per cent of GDP (£144bn per year)"
> "a jobs apocalypse is not inevitable – government, employers and unions have the opportunity"
> — [IPPR, 27 Mar 2024](https://www.ippr.org/media-office/up-to-8-million-uk-jobs-at-risk-from-ai-unless-government-acts-finds-ippr)

IPPR's worst case is 7.9 million jobs lost. It found back-office, entry-level and part-time roles most exposed first, with **higher-paid cognitive work exposed in a second wave** as exposure could rise to 59% of tasks. Women and young people were more exposed.

**For the Beacon Party's focus on middle-income earners**, the evidence points two ways:
- The first wave hits administrative, junior and clerical roles.
- Morgan Stanley's mid-career finding, and the second-wave modelling, put the core **middle-income professional and white-collar workforce** squarely in scope over the next several years.

### B3. Does retraining work?

- The government's main answer is skills. In June 2026 it announced a partnership with Nvidia, Google, Microsoft and others to give **7.5 million workers** essential AI skills by 2030. It also announced a DWP "AI Work Assistant" ("job centre in your pocket") on a three-month trial ([HR Review](https://hrreview.co.uk/hr-news/jobs-labour-market/job-centre-in-your-pocket-plan-raises-questions-over-role-of-ai/388454); [The Register](https://theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/06/08/uks-answer-to-ai-job-fears-is-a-bot-to-polish-your-cv/5251983)).
- Critics noted this is a CV and job-search tool, not income support.
- In an April 2026 Lords debate, a peer cited Ipsos research that 84% of people in work had done no AI training in the previous year ([Hansard, Lords, 13 Apr 2026](https://hansard.parliament.uk/lords/2026-04-13/debates/5D5AAA0E-BE76-4BA1-877F-25D32337BECE/ArtificialIntelligenceImpactOnEmployment)).
- **The historical lesson** is that retraining alone did not rescue former coal, steel and manufacturing communities in the 1980s–90s. Income and place-based investment mattered as much as skills. *This research did not find a 2026 UK evaluation measuring whether AI retraining leads to equivalent jobs. That is a gap to fill.*
- The key structural difference this time is that retraining assumes there is a *destination* job. If AI keeps advancing across cognitive work, the destination keeps moving. This is the core of the Discontinuity Thesis argument (see F2).

### B4. The government's position is shifting

- **Early 2026:** Treasury minister Dan Tomlinson said of past technology shocks: "You don't see job losses overall" † ([Nation.Cymru](https://nation.cymru/news/minister-plays-down-job-losses-as-chancellor-prepares-to-commit-to-ai-drive)).
- **September 2026:** the new AI minister, Kanishka Narayan, in Cabinet under PM Andy Burnham:

> "Britain is preparing for the possibility of "unprecedented" AI-driven job losses"
> "He stressed there was currently no evidence that AI had caused an overall fall in UK employment"
> — [City AM, 29 Sep 2026](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2461239)

- **Budget 2026 (28 October):** Chancellor John Healey:

> "I understand and share the concerns of working people, so I am setting out a distinctively British approach to AI"
> "The Treasury will commit £10m a year to studying the impact of AI on employment"
> — [City AM, 24 Oct 2026](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2471048)

The largest items in the package were security-related (£165m for a National AI Biosecurity Initiative; £120m for AISI).

- **November 2026:** the Treasury is reportedly examining whether an AI tax or wider reforms could replace lost income-tax revenue. This rests on an unconfirmed Telegraph report † ([The Employment Law Solicitors](https://www.theemploymentlawsolicitors.co.uk/news/2026/11/15/ai-tax-3/)).

- **Labour's Darren Jones MP, a former Cabinet Office minister,** has warned of the fiscal and political consequences:

> "continued AI development and widespread business adoption could create 'cracks in the very foundations' of the UK economy"
> "governments could face a 'populist backlash' if they fail to prepare workers and communities"
> — [IBTimes UK, 21 Sep 2026](https://ibtimes.co.uk/darren-jones-warns-ai-could-leave-britain-without-enough-jobs-1820859)

**Takeaway:** the government now accepts the risk, but has no published plan for income support during displacement.

## C. Value drain and UK strengths

### C1. Where the money goes

> "less than £1 in every £6 spent with its biggest technology suppliers goes to British firms"
> "accounting for £9.2 billion of the £19.2 billion spent with the largest suppliers."
> — [The Register, 11 Nov 2026](https://www.theregister.com/public-sector/2026/11/11/uks-buy-british-tech-ambitions-hit-reality-as-less-than-1-in-6-stays-at-home/5308814) (US firms are the biggest beneficiaries)

- A Freedom of Information response found that 10 of the 11 AI tools the Cabinet Office disclosed depend on models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or Microsoft ([Computing](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2026/government/uk-s-sovereign-ai-overwhelmingly-dependent-on-us-tech)).
- The Liberal Democrats put it bluntly:

> "For too long, the UK has been content to be a digital colony of Silicon Valley."
> — [Liberal Democrats, 22 Apr 2026](https://www.libdems.org.uk/press/release/lib-dems-announce-plan-for-ai-digital-independence-to-end-the-uks-dependence-on-us-tech-giants)

- **The fiscal mechanism of "drain":** as work moves from UK employees, who pay income tax and NICs, to AI services billed by overseas firms, the UK tax base erodes. Corporation tax doesn't make up the difference, because profits are booked elsewhere. RAND's US analysis found corporate tax rates would need roughly to **double** to replace lost labour-tax revenue ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4980-1.html)). The UK, as a *buyer* rather than a *builder* of frontier AI, would be worse off.

### C2. Infrastructure setbacks

> "pressed pause on its Stargate UK data center project over rising energy costs and regulatory uncertainty"
> — [TechRadar, 10 Apr 2026](https://www.techradar.com/pro/openai-halts-gbp31-billion-stargate-uk-project-over-rising-energy-costs-and-regulatory-deadlock)

UK industrial electricity prices are among the highest in the IEA. One analysis puts them at more than four times those in the US and Nordic countries ([Energy Digital](https://energydigital.com/news/why-high-energy-prices-have-halted-openais-uk-stargate-plan)). **Energy is an AI policy.**

### C3. UK strengths and current investment

> "One year on, we have now met our commitments against 38 of the 50 actions"
> "generating £28.2 billion in investment, creating more than 15,000 jobs"
> "is backed by up to £500 million of government funding to invest in and support UK AI companies."
> — [DSIT, *AI Opportunities Action Plan: One Year On*, 29 Jan 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-opportunities-action-plan-one-year-on/ai-opportunities-action-plan-one-year-on)

- **Compute:** £2bn to grow public compute twentyfold by 2030; Isambard-AI in Bristol.
- **AI Growth Zones:** Oxfordshire, South Wales, North Wales, North East England, Lanarkshire. The independent commentator CASRAI describes much of this as "designated but not yet delivered" †.
- **Sector size:** AI-related revenues rose 41% to £33.6bn in the government's 2025 sector study ([DSIT AI Sector Study 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/artificial-intelligence-sector-study-2025/artificial-intelligence-sector-study-2025-web-version)).
- **Budget 2026:** about £700m in AI measures, including a sovereign AI element ([Technology Magazine](https://technologymagazine.com/news/budget-2026-uk-unveils-700m-fund-for-sovereign-ai)).
- **Concern over conditions:** a New Statesman report, based on an advocacy group's FOI, says more than one in three companies backed by the Sovereign AI fund are US-owned, with no conditions attached † ([New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/spotlight/tech-regulation/artificial-intelligence/2026/07/who-owns-the-future)).

**Areas where the UK is distinctively strong.** These are widely recognised, though this research didn't gather fresh 2026 sector figures for each:
- AI safety and security evaluation. AISI is the world's best-resourced government AI evaluator.
- AI assurance and audit services.
- Life sciences and health data (the NHS).
- Financial services and fintech.
- Creative industries.
- Elite university research (Oxford, Cambridge, Edinburgh, Imperial, UCL).
- Chip design (Arm), plus a growing applied-AI startup scene.

**Implication for an Investment Beacon:** the UK can't outspend the US on frontier models. A credible target is to **own stakes in, and build sovereign capacity around, the layers where the UK can win**: safety and assurance, health AI, fintech AI, specialised compute and energy. Public money should come with **equity or revenue-share conditions** so returns flow back to citizens (see D2).

## D. Supporting people through the transition

### D1. Income support options and evidence

**Universal Basic Income: costs**
- The IFS estimates that paying every working-age adult the basic rate of Universal Credit (~£400/month) would cost **over £200bn a year gross**. The IFS page itself returned an error when fetched, so this is from secondary reporting † ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2453487); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/articles/economics-universal-basic-income)).
- Peer-reviewed microsimulation puts the **net** cost of a poverty-level UBI at about **£45bn/year (~2% of GDP)**. The gap is because most recipients also pay tax ([Widerquist & Arndt](https://widerquist.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/The-Cost-of-Basic-Income-in-the-United-Kingdom-A-Microsimulation-Analysis-119.pdf)).

**Pilot evidence**
- **US OpenResearch study (2020–23):**

> "For three years, 1,000 people received $1,000 per month—no strings attached."
> "worked about 1.3 fewer hours a week than those who got $50"
> — [Route Fifty / The 19th, 23 Jul 2024](https://www.route-fifty.com/management/2024/07/results-biggest-study-guaranteed-income-programs-are-finally/398260/)

  The employment effect was modest (about 2 percentage points), with some recipients moving into education ([NBER w32719](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32719/w32719.pdf)).
- **Wales Basic Income for Care Leavers** (£1,600/month gross for 24 months):
  - Self-reported improvements in wellbeing and reduced financial stress.
  - The February 2026 report is "the first report published since the pilot has ended and all young people have exited the scheme" † ([Welsh Government](https://gov.wales/written-statement-basic-income-care-leavers-wales-pilot-evaluation-annual-report-2025-2026)).
  - Impact and value-for-money results are due in 2027.

**Government signals**

> "we're going to have to think really carefully about how we soft-land those industries that go away"
> — Lord Stockwood, investment minister, quoted in [Fortune, 1 Feb 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/02/01/elon-musk-optional-work-fantasy-universal-basic-income-uk-minister-jason-stockwood/)

Stockwood also floated a windfall levy on the firms that benefit most from AI. UBI is not government policy.

**Options for displaced middle-income workers**, which is closest to the human's priority:

| Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wage insurance / transition income** | Tops up income for a fixed period if a new job pays less, or pays a high share of previous salary while retraining | Targets displaced middle earners and protects mortgages and families | Needs a definition of "AI-displaced"; time-limited |
| **Enhanced Universal Credit** | Higher rates, softer conditions and savings taper | Uses an existing system | UC is a low floor; feels punitive to middle earners |
| **Job guarantee** | Public-interest jobs at a living wage | Keeps work and dignity | Hard to scale; may not suit professionals |
| **UBI / UHI** | Unconditional payment for all | Simple; no stigma; future-proof | Expensive now; politically hard before job losses are visible |
| **Universal Basic Services** | Free transport, care, digital, housing support | Lowers cost of living; efficient | Doesn't replace income |

### D3. Paying for it

- **The fiscal starting point is tight.** The OBR's spring 2026 forecast had public sector net debt at **94.3% of GDP in 2025-26**, peaking at **96.3% in 2028-29** ([OBR](https://obr.uk/brief-guides/public-finances/)).
- **Options:**
  - Windfall levies on AI-driven excess profits.
  - Taxes on compute or AI usage (per-token levies are being debated in the US).
  - A stronger digital services tax.
  - Shifting tax from labour towards capital, land and consumption (VAT was suggested by the [Tax Policy Center](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefs/taxing-artificial-intelligence)).
  - **Public equity stakes in return for public investment**, which is the most politically attractive because it is "ownership, not just tax".
- **Key insight from Oliver Ritchie (Future of Life Institute UK policy lead)** in *The Fiscal Challenge of Advanced AI*: the question isn't only whether we *can* pay displaced workers, but whether the state keeps a tax base to do so. This makes taxing or owning AI-generated value a **precondition**, not an add-on.

> "52% think AI's overall impact will be negative, up from 34% in 2024"
> "79% have little to no confidence in AI companies to develop the technology responsibly"
> — [YouGov, 18 Sep 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55587-britons-are-increasingly-worried-about-the-impact-of-ai)

- YouGov also found **80%** have little or no confidence in current and future UK governments to regulate AI effectively †.
- A commercially commissioned ICS.AI survey (July 2026) found 49% think ministers aren't moving fast enough, and 58% think the UK is not well prepared for AI's impact on jobs † ([Compare the Cloud](https://www.comparethecloud.net/news/icsai-survey-half-of-britons-say-government-is-not-moving-fast-enough-on-ai)).
- A City AM/Freshwater Strategy poll found ~67% believe AI will replace jobs faster than it creates them † ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/labour-voters-lead-ai-adoption-as-public-remains-split-on-impact/)).

> **A caution on reading these polls (added in v1.4).** Most AI polling asks people about **opinions or hypotheticals**: "Are you worried?", "Would you support slowing AI down?", "Would you back a basic income?". As Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* argues, people asked what they think of an idea tend to answer politely or optimistically, and guesses about future behaviour are unreliable. Evidence about **what has already happened** is much stronger, for example "A fifth of employers (22%) have already made roles redundant or reduced hiring because of AI" (KCL). The figures above show the *direction* of public mood, not how people will vote. The founders' notes (004, section 2) therefore recommend **100+ listening conversations** about people's real experiences, feelings, worries and hopes, *before* deciding the party's priorities. That is the aim: understanding people well enough to build the party around them, not finding words to sell it.

**The Beacon Party's target audience** is people growing more concerned about AI who don't see government doing enough. The polling suggests this is already a **majority sentiment**, not a niche.

## H. Single-issue parties, recruiting talent, and "not a tech party"

### H1. UKIP and the Brexit Party/Reform (item 18)

- **UKIP's growth:** UKIP (founded 1991–93) was dismissed for years as a single-issue party. It grew by tying its core issue (the EU) to a wider everyday concern (immigration), and by **pressuring the Conservatives**. Cameron's 2013 referendum pledge is widely read as a response to the UKIP threat ([QMUL, Bale](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/bitstream/handle/123456789/32065/Bale%20Who%20leads%20and%20who%20follows%3F%20The%20symbiotic%20relationship%20between%20UKIP%20and%20the%20Conservatives%20%E2%80%93%20and%20populism%20and%20Euroscepticism%202018%20Accepted.pdf?sequence=1)).
- **Then success killed it:**

> "Brexit marked UKIP's high point and moment of triumph, yet it also sealed the party's doom."
> — [Australian Outlook, 12 Jun 2017](https://www.internationalaffairs.org.au/australianoutlook/life-death-ukip/)

- **Reform's survival:** the Brexit Party won the 2019 European elections but no Westminster seats. As **Reform UK (renamed November 2020)** it expanded beyond a single issue, and has since attracted a stream of experienced defectors. By January 2026 these included sitting MPs Robert Jenrick, Andrew Rosindell and Suella Braverman, and multiple former cabinet ministers ([Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Conservative_Party_defections_to_Reform_UK)).

**Lessons for the Beacon Party:**
1. A single-issue party can **change national policy without winning power**, by threatening the big parties' votes. That alone could be a success.
2. **Connect AI to everyday concerns** (cost of living, the NHS, housing, young people's prospects), as UKIP connected the EU to immigration.
3. **AI is unlike Brexit: it doesn't end.** It is a decades-long transition that touches every department. This gives an AI party a **permanent purpose**, and the obligation to govern everything else well.
4. Have a **broad platform for non-AI issues** from the start, staffed by people with real experience.

### H2. Attracting experienced politicians (item 18a)

- **SDP (1981):** four senior Labour figures left and drew 28 MPs. Despite strong polling, the Alliance won only 23 seats in 1983 under first-past-the-post. It eventually merged into the Liberal Democrats ([University of Birmingham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/research/perspective/lessons-to-learn-from-the-sdp-for-the-independents)).
- **Change UK (2019):** 11 MPs, unclear purpose, collapsed within months:

> "the eleven MPs are less recognisable than the Social Democrats' leaders"
> — [University of Birmingham, Dr Matt Cole](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/research/perspective/lessons-to-learn-from-the-sdp-for-the-independents)

- **Reform (2024–26):** defectors came because Reform was **already polling strongly** and offered a clear direction. Polling success came first and attracted the talent.
- **Your Party (2025–26):** a famous founder and 55,000 members, but it split within a year over leadership ([QMUL](https://www.qmul.ac.uk/mei/news-and-opinion/items/long-read-a-year-of-your-party.html)).

**Lessons:**
- Experienced people join a party that has **momentum, a clear purpose and governance that works**.
- **Recruit beyond politicians:** retired civil servants, local government leaders, NHS managers, business leaders and economists, who can run the "normal" departments credibly.
- **Avoid personality-driven splits** by writing clear governance and conflict-resolution rules from day one.

### H3. "Not a tech party" (item 18b)

- **Team Mirai** shows what a *pro-technology* party looks like: digital democracy, faster AI adoption.
- **ControlAI, PauseAI and Guardrails** show the *anti/slow-down* end.
- **The public sits in between:** 61% would support slowing AI down "but still being allowed to continue" (YouGov).
- **The Beacon Party's distinctive position:**

  > *"The transformation is coming regardless. We are not here to speed it up or stop it. We are here to make sure Britain gets through it fairly, safely and together."*

  This is a **politics-of-transition** stance, not a technology stance. It is like a party that neither caused nor opposes an economic shock but has the best plan for steering through it. It also avoids being captured by industry money (cf. Leading the Future vs Guardrails in the US).

### H4. Fairness with merit (item 19)

- **The human's value:** people who work hard or are more able should still be able to do better, *and* everyone should share safely in AI's abundance.
- **The evidence base for "floor plus ladder" designs:**
  - **A floor:** income guarantee or dividend, plus basic services, so nobody falls through.
  - **A ladder:** wages, enterprise and ownership still reward effort and talent.
  - Dividends from a public AI fund are a **shared inheritance**, like Alaska's, not a wage. So they don't remove the reward for work.
  - The OpenResearch results suggest unconditional cash causes only small reductions in work.

## J. International coordination: what the UK can and can't do alone

*Added in version 1.1, after the human pointed out that acting alone on some measures could be economic suicide.*

### J1. The risk of acting alone

AI investment moves easily between countries, and the UK is mostly a **buyer** of frontier AI, not a builder (see C1). Three findings show the cost of going it alone:

- **Investment leaves.** Stargate UK (£31bn) was paused over "the cost of energy, which has become the highest in Europe, and regulatory uncertainty" ([TechRadar, 10 Apr 2026](https://www.techradar.com/pro/openai-halts-gbp31-billion-stargate-uk-project-over-rising-energy-costs-and-regulatory-deadlock)).
- **Unilateral taxes provoke retaliation.** The UK's Digital Services Tax raises only about £800m a year, yet it has triggered US tariff threats, including a threatened 100% tariff in April 2026 † ([Express & Star](https://www.expressandstar.com/uk-news/donald-trump-threatens-100-tariff-on-uk-over-digital-tax-on-us-tech-firms-8764868)). It also put a tech partnership on hold:

> "has put a proposed $42 billion (£31 billion) trade pact with the UK on ice"
> — [The Register, 16 Dec 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/16/us_uk_trade_deal/)

  The government has reportedly said it would replace the DST with "a global solution" once one exists † ([TechPolicy.Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/the-ukus-tech-deal-exposes-the-limits-of-britains-ai-ambitions/)).
- **Rival parties will use this argument.** Badenoch argues AI firms "won't come here" if Britain over-regulates ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2440509)). A Beacon plan without an international strategy is open to exactly this attack.

### J2. Precedents: coordination works, but it's fragile

**OECD global minimum corporate tax (Pillar Two).** Around 140 countries agreed a 15% minimum tax on large multinationals in 2021. In January 2026 a "side-by-side" deal:

> "effectively exempts US multinationals from most BEPS 2.0 Pillar Two global minimum tax rules"
> — [EY, 5 Jan 2026](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-0115-breaking-tax-news-oecd-releases-side-by-side-arrangement-exempting-us-multinationals-from-most-pillar-two-global-minimum-tax-rules)

**Lesson:** global tax deals *can* be agreed, but the US (home to most frontier AI companies) can carve itself out. Countries can still apply **domestic top-up taxes** on profits earned in their territory, which gives a fallback.

**The Montreal Protocol (1987), which protected the ozone layer.** It is widely seen as the most successful environmental treaty. It worked through a **club model**: trade restrictions on non-members removed the advantage of staying out, and financial help for developing countries brought them in ([Stimson Center](https://stimson.org/2024/the-remarkable-story-of-the-montreal-protocol-with-lessons-for-cyberspace/)). Analysts debate whether a full trade ban is practical for AI. Conditions on market access, such as "to sell frontier AI services here, meet these standards and pay these levies", are the AI equivalent.

**Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI.** This is the first legally binding international AI treaty, covering human rights, democracy and the rule of law. The UK, the EU and the US signed it in 2024. The EU has ratified it. No evidence was found of UK ratification ([Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/-/european-union-ratifies-the-council-of-europe-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)).

### J3. The current international landscape (November 2026)

**The UK's G20 presidency (December 2026 to November 2027) is a big opening.**

> "Britain will try to broker a worldwide deal on AI during its upcoming turn at the head of the G20"
> — [The Next Web, 22 Sep 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/andy-burnham-uk-g20-global-ai-agreement)

> "culminating in a proposed "Manchester Compact on AI" at next November's G20 leaders' summit"
> — [City AM, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2479080)

The summit is planned for Manchester on 27–28 November 2027 †.

**A coalition of the willing already exists.** Finland and Norway led a declaration at the UN General Assembly (September 2026):

> "A declaration signed by 22 countries calls for AI to remain under "human direction, oversight and control""
> "Neither the United States nor China signed the document."
> — [FAIR EDIH, 22 Sep 2026](https://www.fairedih.fi/en/2026/09/22/finland-and-norway-lead-22-nation-call-for-global-ai-oversight-body-as-us-and-china-stay-out/)

The UK also didn't sign, which drew criticism. The declaration calls for mandatory pre-deployment testing and explores a new international oversight institution. Finland's President has suggested one modelled on the IAEA.

**But the G20 currently leans "light-touch".** In September 2026, under the US presidency, G20 innovation ministers adopted voluntary "AI Prosperity Objectives" focused on workforce skills:

> "governments should be cautious about creating entirely new regulatory systems for AI"
> — [TechRepublic, 4 Sep 2026](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-g20-us-ai-regulation-principles/)

**The UN track:**
- An Independent International Scientific Panel on AI (co-chairs Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa) published a preliminary report in July 2026.
- The first UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance met in Geneva in July 2026. The next is in New York in 2027 ([UN](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)).
- A proposed **Global Fund on AI** (about US$3bn initially) for developing countries is recommended by the UN Secretary-General, but has not been established † ([UN A/79/966](https://www.un.org/digital-emerging-technologies/sites/www.un.org.digital-emerging-technologies/files/A_79_966-EN.pdf)).

**Global sharing of gains:** the Windfall Trust's global dividend proposal (see D2) is the most developed model for sharing AI wealth across nations. It is still only a proposal.

### J4. What the UK can do alone, and what needs coordination

| Safe for the UK to do alone | Needs international coordination (or risks investment leaving) |
|---|---|
| Bridge: Transition Income, retraining, home protection (domestic spending) | AI windfall levies and compute or usage taxes on global providers |
| Equity in return for *UK public* investment | Binding licensing and pre-deployment testing of frontier models |
| Buy-British public AI procurement | Minimum AI corporate tax (closing the gap left by the Pillar Two carve-out) |
| AISI testing; deepfake election law | A global dividend or income floor for poorer nations |
| A citizens' fund holding the UK's own stakes | Preventing a race to the bottom on safety and tax |
| Domestic top-up tax on UK-earned profits (Pillar Two-style) | Common standards so firms can't play countries off against each other |

**Conclusion:** the research supports a dedicated **Global Treaty Beacon**:
- The UK should **go first where it's safe** to act alone.
- It should use its **2027 G20 presidency** and the existing coalition led by Finland and Norway to build a club of willing countries on the rest.
- The club needs **market-access conditions**, as in the Montreal Protocol model, so staying outside isn't a free ride.
- It needs a **fallback**: a bloc large enough to set terms (for example the EU, the UK and allies) if the US and China refuse.

## Synthesis: the gap, and what it means for the Beacons

### The gap

1. **The risk is officially acknowledged, but nobody owns the plan.** The government studies (£10m/yr) and contingency-plans, but publishes no income-support plan for displaced workers.
2. **No party makes the AI transition its central purpose.** Each covers a slice: Lab (adoption and skills), Con/Reform (deregulation), LD (sovereignty), Green (regulation/ownership).
3. **The ideas exist, but they are scattered** across IPPR, the TUC, the Windfall Trust, FLI and the UBI Labs.
4. **Public mood is ripe:** 70% worried, 7% expect fair sharing, 80% lack confidence in government regulation, 9% trust politicians.
5. **Trust is the scarcest resource.** A party that publishes measurable Beacons and openly reports changes would be **structurally different** from the parties voters distrust.

## Open questions and limits of this research

- **Primary-source gaps:**
  - The IFS UBI page (403 error) and the Wales evaluation PDF couldn't be read directly.
  - Ted Shelton's paper and the full text of the January 2026 DSIT assessment weren't read in full.
- **Unverified items marked †:** these come from search-engine excerpts and should be checked before publication.
- **Party policy:** detailed AI-and-jobs positions for SNP, Plaid and Reform's forthcoming skills policy weren't found.
- **The retraining evidence base** for AI-displaced professionals is thin. No UK evaluation was found.
- **Costs:** the Beacon costings above are illustrative. Proper costing (e.g. Transition Income at different take-up levels) is needed.
- **International (section J):** the UN Global Fund on AI summary page (dig.watch) returned an error. China's stance and the EU AI Act's "Brussels effect" were not researched in depth.
- **No qualitative listening research yet:** this document relies on desk research and polling. It contains no first-hand conversations with members of the public about their experiences of AI. See 004, section 2.
- **Fast-moving facts:** political facts (PM, ministers, poll numbers) are as of November 2026 and change quickly.
